## 1. Selection determination

This is a genuine **Tech From Here** candidate. Its subject is a hybrid custody system that makes one shipment exist in two coupled forms:

- a physical load moving in an inspected, unmarked vehicle;
- a digital transfer described in a seed-to-sale manifest;
- registered workers whose identities bind people to the trip;
- locked, concealed cargo and an alarmed vehicle;
- origin, destination, product, quantity, vehicle, and estimated-time fields;
- observed loading and unloading;
- exception records for unscheduled stops, crashes, breakdowns, loss, theft, damaged goods, and refusals; and
- receiving-side records that reconcile what was expected with what arrived.

Nevada is not merely the road beneath the vehicle. Nevada's regulatory architecture specifies which physical and informational controls must travel together, and its 2020 emergency response briefly made remote vehicle inspection part of the supply-chain interface.

The original “Invisible Convoy” framing is directionally strong but overstates the technology if it includes GPS or onboard video as universal current requirements. The public record reviewed here supports seed-to-sale manifests, inspected vehicles, locked concealed cargo, unmarked exteriors, alarms, communication capability, surveillance of loading/unloading, and exception reporting. It does **not** yet support a claim that every Nevada distributor vehicle must use GPS or an onboard camera.

## 2. Corrected story promise

### Recommended working title

**The Cannabis Van Is Supposed to Disappear. Its Data Cannot.**

### Evidence-safe thesis

Nevada makes wholesale cannabis transport physically inconspicuous while requiring the trip to remain legible to a seed-to-sale and custody system. The vehicle carries no cannabis markings and the load stays locked and hidden, but the manifest identifies the establishments, products, quantities, vehicle, workers, and expected timing. When the trip deviates—through a stop, crash, breakdown, loss, theft, refusal, or damage—the deviation generates another record. The technology is not a “smart van”; it is the coupling of ordinary vehicle hardware, access controls, human identity, surveillance at handoff, and a digital manifest.

### LinkedIn-native revelation

The safest-looking cannabis transport vehicle in Nevada may look like nothing at all. That invisibility is designed. What makes the trip accountable is the parallel record: who has the load, what is in it, which vehicle is carrying it, where it should go, when it should arrive, and what must be logged when reality departs from the plan.

### Saveable payload

**The Seven-Link Cannabis Custody Test**

1. **Declare:** Does the digital manifest identify the load, parties, vehicle, workers, and timing?
2. **Secure:** Is the physical cargo locked, concealed, sanitary, and protected for its product type?
3. **Authenticate:** Are the transporter, vehicle, origin, and destination authorized?
4. **Observe:** Are loading and unloading captured at the licensed handoff points?
5. **Communicate:** Can the worker reach the responsible establishment during transport?
6. **Exception:** Are stops, crashes, breakdowns, loss, theft, damage, and refusals recorded and routed correctly?
7. **Reconcile:** Do the receiving record, physical inventory, and seed-to-sale transfer agree?

This is an editorial framework, not an official CCB checklist.

## 3. Documented mechanism

### A. Digital manifest before movement

Nevada's Regulation 13 requires a physical or electronic transportation manifest generated through the seed-to-sale system. Located effective text specifies origin, destination, distributor, products and quantities, expected departure and arrival, vehicle make/model/license plate and approval-card number, and the names and registration-card numbers of the transporting agents. A multi-stop trip must keep inventory for each destination distinct.

### B. Physical concealment and access control

The transporting worker must carry the manifest and agent credential. Cannabis must be in a sanitary, secure lockbox or locked cargo area, not visible from outside. The vehicle may not carry cannabis-related signs or markings. Separate vehicle provisions require Board approval and an identification card and specify an audible car alarm. Perishable products add temperature-control duties.

### C. Handoff observation and record adjustment

Loading and unloading must occur within view of a licensed establishment's video-surveillance system. The originating and receiving establishments have separate inventory duties, and the distributor may not rewrite origin-entered transfer information. This creates a meaningful trust boundary: the transporter moves the load but does not own every authoritative field describing it.

### D. Exceptions are part of the system

Current located text requires notice or reports for unauthorized stops, crashes or interrupted travel, loss, and theft; damaged or refused product returns to origin. Revised proposed R152-24RP1 would change some reporting deadlines and language. The proposal is useful for identifying live design questions but must not be cited as current law unless final adoption and effective codification are verified immediately before drafting.

### E. Nevada's emergency virtual-inspection episode

In March 2020, Nevada moved legal retail cannabis sales to delivery-only status and implemented virtual delivery-vehicle inspections to meet increased demand. This is a potentially vivid Nevada-specific historical turn: the state changed the inspection interface, not the underlying need for an approved vehicle. It should appear only if the final article distinguishes retail delivery from wholesale distribution and confirms exactly how the virtual procedure worked.

## 5. Barred claims

The canonical article, LinkedIn post, selected visual, captions, and promotional copy must not state or imply that:

- every Nevada cannabis transport van is armored, GPS-tracked, geofenced, or continuously filmed;
- regulators watch each trip live on a map;
- an unmarked vehicle is inherently secure;
- Metrc proves that the physical load always matches the digital manifest;
- a manifest is a blockchain record;
- the transporter's inability to alter origin-entered fields prevents every form of fraud or mistake;
- retail consumer delivery and wholesale establishment-to-establishment distribution follow identical rules;
- the March 2020 virtual-inspection procedure remains the current standard;
- proposed R152-24RP1 deadlines are effective law; or
- publishing routes, vehicle plates, worker credentials, lock locations, security configurations, or current schedules is justified by the story.

## 9. Duplication and series-fit audit

This candidate is distinct from the plant/package data-double and recall stories. Those examine identity over a product's lifecycle and reverse traceability after a hazard. This story examines custody while the product is physically between licensed premises: the handoff among manifest, worker, vehicle, cargo, and receiving record.

Its main risk is becoming a compliance checklist. The canonical article needs people and conflict: drivers working inside a system built around distrust; the mismatch between physical invisibility and digital legibility; 2020's rapid virtual-inspection change; and what happens when a load, record, clock, vehicle, or receiving party does not behave as planned.
