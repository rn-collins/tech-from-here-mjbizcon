## Story 2 — controlled access chronology

| Date/layer | Official record establishes | Article boundary |
|---|---|---|
| 1998 and 2000 elections | Nevada voters approved Question 9 twice; Article 4, section 38 requires legislative provision for specified medical use and a registry. | A constitutional command is not a retail supply system. |
| 2001 | AB 453 added former NRS chapter 453A, creating the patient-registry/prosecution-exemption framework; contemporaneous legislative history also records NAC chapter 453A implementation. | Early law included possession/cultivation/caregiver mechanisms. Never write “legal protection but no access of any kind.” |
| 2009 | The program transferred from Agriculture to the Health Division. | Administrative ownership changed before commercial establishments existed. |
| 11 June 2013 | SB 374 became law and created medical-marijuana establishments: dispensaries, cultivation, production, and independent testing. | Enactment was not store opening. |
| 5 August 2014 application phase | Legislative testimony records the department's start of establishment application review. | Application review was not final certification, local approval, inspection, inventory, or operation. |
| 2015 operating phase | Official fiscal/legislative records describe the establishment program and its licensed entity types. | A contemporaneous state/local record is still required before naming a single “first Nevada dispensary” and exact opening day. |

**Draftable thesis:** Nevada separated constitutional recognition, patient registration, establishment authorization, and practical storefront access into different institutional events spread across years.

**Prohibited thesis:** Nevada patients had a constitutional right to buy cannabis beginning in 2000, or had no legally relevant supply mechanism until 2015.

**Remaining release work:** locate the first final certificates, local approvals, inspection/opening records, patient geography, registered-patient counts, and contemporaneous patient accounts; legal cite-check immediately before publication.

## Story 3 — potency-enforcement docket

### Verified public record

- The CCB's 2025 Biennial Report says agents investigated THC-potency practices across all Nevada laboratories.
- It says suspected inflating laboratories were identified and the practice was later curbed.
- The report juxtaposes 2020 and 2023 histograms and states later histograms remained consistent with the 2020 distribution.
- It describes flower in the 20–25% range as a general average and says results above 30% trigger review.
- Other CCB records show that potency, laboratory practices, raw chromatograms, Metrc documentation, retesting, and remediation can enter compliance or disciplinary proceedings. These records do not supply a public laboratory-by-laboratory causal dataset for the paired histograms.

### What the evidence can and cannot do

| May be drafted | Still prohibited |
|---|---|
| Nevada used the shape of aggregate laboratory results to screen for anomalies and direct review. | The histograms independently prove fraud or prove that enforcement caused the later shape. |
| A >30% result was a regulator-described review trigger. | 30% is a biological ceiling or automatic violation. |
| Distributional monitoring is one layer beside raw-data review, chain of custody, retesting, and investigation. | The chart identifies which laboratory or sample was wrong. |
| The regulator publicly characterized suspected inflation as curbed. | Every laboratory corrected its conduct or product labels became accurate. |

**Missing docket:** machine-readable bins, denominators, exclusions, laboratory universe, dry-weight/rounding rules, duplicate/retest handling, trigger workflow, named case linkage, final sanctions, and counterfactual/product-mix controls.

**Draft form authorized:** a transparent “how a regulator reads a histogram” investigation with the missing-data problem as a central finding. Analytical-chemist and statistician review remains a release gate.

## Story 10 — Clark County's support-business perimeter

### Directly established by the county packet

- Applies within **unincorporated Clark County**, not the incorporated City of Las Vegas or Nevada statewide.
- Trigger language: a business “whose purpose is to provide ancillary support to Cannabis Establishments, or the Cannabis industry at large” must apply for the county license.
- The packet requests a detailed service description, ownership and investment disclosure, organization/affiliate material, leases/property documentation, and a privileged regulated-license financial questionnaire.
- The department may request an FBI background check; a CCB agent card may substitute.
- Public-safety inspections/signoffs precede a code conference; the applicant must present a business plan; no business may operate until the license issues.
- The listed application/license amount is $70 in the current packet, but current fees must be recaptured at publication.

### Perimeter crosswalk

| Question | Status |
|---|---|
| Does a dedicated non-plant-touching category exist? | **Verified.** |
| Does it reach every lawyer, accountant, landlord, software company, advertiser, cleaner, or supplier with a cannabis client? | **Not established.** “Purpose” and “ancillary support” require code/advisory interpretation. |
| Is it equivalent to state plant-touching licensure? | **No basis.** Compare jurisdiction, trigger, investigation, approval, renewal, enforcement, and appeal before drawing equivalence. |
| Does the packet impose a suitability-style information burden? | **Draftable with specifics**, not as a blanket gaming-law label: ownership, finance, background, inspection, and operational review are documented. |
| How many applied, were approved, denied, abandoned, or disciplined? | **Open.** Requires county administrative data/public-record request. |

**Draft form authorized:** an examination of the application architecture and the question it raises—how far cannabis regulation extends into the service ecosystem. Market prevalence or burden conclusions remain blocked.

### Defensible cross-document finding

The agreements are not transfers of tribal licensing power to Nevada. They are interoperability instruments that coordinate defined cross-border commerce through tracking, delivery verification, state-facing testing/packaging/labeling rules, fiscal/audit arrangements, notice, inspection/cooperation, and dispute/termination provisions while preserving jurisdictional limits and sovereignty language. The common architecture is real; equivalence is not.

### Interpretation prohibitions and gates

- Do not infer a nation's motives, priorities, benefits, harms, or community consensus from compact text.
- Do not describe sovereignty as a “loophole,” or state-system participation as the surrender of sovereignty.
- Do not merge twelve governments into a generic “tribal cannabis” actor.
- Verify operative status, amendments, superseding agreements, and signatory authority with the nation and state.
- Obtain nation-specific review before interpretive drafting or publication; avoid restricted cultural knowledge.
