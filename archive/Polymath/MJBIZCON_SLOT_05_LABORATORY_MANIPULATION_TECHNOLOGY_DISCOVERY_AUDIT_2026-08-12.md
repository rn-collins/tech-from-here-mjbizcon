# MJBizCon 2026 Canonical 14 — Slot 5 Laboratory Manipulation Technology-Discovery Audit

**Audit date:** 12 August 2026 HST  
**Provisional proposition entering audit:** *A Laboratory Could Inflate THC and Still Produce an Official-Looking Certificate*  
**Case:** State of Nevada Cannabis Compliance Board v. Cannex Nevada, LLC, now known as LettuceTest, LLC, Case No. 2020-27  
**Decision:** **PASS PROVISIONALLY AS AN EXCEPTIONAL, DISTINCT TECHNOLOGY STORY; DOSSIER AND DRAFTING REMAIN BLOCKED.**

## Controlling editorial finding

This story survives the required broad discovery pass. It is unusually strong because the technology is not a generic laboratory instrument or a morality tale about a dishonest number. The central technology is the complete **sample-to-decision-to-certificate control system**: physical samples and aliquots; approved methods and standard operating procedures; quality controls; retesting authorization and invalid-result rules; trend logs and other records; human result selection; reporting to the state; and the certificate of analysis that turns laboratory work into an official market decision.

The Nevada Cannabis Compliance Board's 4 March 2024 final order provides a primary, adjudicated record. The Board found, among other violations, that the laboratory performed unauthorized retesting, did not report all results, lacked a defensible method for choosing which potency result to report, and used practices directed toward increasing reported potency and avoiding lower results. It also found misconduct involving microbial and cadmium failures. The Board imposed a total $57,500 civil penalty and a 180-day suspension, not revocation.

This is not permission to repeat every complaint allegation. The final order records findings, split votes, rejected allegations, respondent arguments and evidentiary limits. The current CCB license record identifies LettuceTest LLC's laboratory license L006 as active, with a 30 June 2027 expiration date as of this audit. Publication must not imply permanent closure, revocation, present misconduct, or that every allegation was proved.

## Best bounded proposition

**Working headline:** *A Laboratory Could Inflate THC and Still Produce an Official-Looking Certificate*  
**Legally precise deck:** Nevada's final order found that a Las Vegas laboratory used unauthorized retesting, failed to report all results and selected potency results that continuously increased—showing how an authoritative certificate depends on the hidden rules and labor behind it.

The word **could** describes a system vulnerability demonstrated by an adjudicated case; it must not become a claim that every Nevada certificate is unreliable. “Official-looking” describes the authority a certificate communicates to readers and the market; the reporting must identify whether the actual case certificates were issued by the laboratory, submitted to the state, displayed through a commercial system, or state-generated. Do not call a laboratory-issued COA a government certificate.

## The required broad four-lens discovery pass

### 1. Place + technology

The meaningful place is not “Las Vegas” used decoratively. Nevada's regulated market made an independent Las Vegas testing laboratory a gate through which cultivation lots became legally reportable results and saleable products. The physical story follows samples from named Nevada cultivators into the laboratory, through preparation and retesting, into laboratory records and certificates, and onward to the state.

The strongest place-specific objects are the case samples, case COAs, trend logs, emails, investigative spreadsheets, SOPs and the rooms in which the investigation and adjudication occurred. Recover the exact historical laboratory address from an official record before assigning it as a location. Third-party listings conflict and are not sufficient. Do not enter or photograph a current laboratory without permission.

### 2. Institution + inventions

The institutional chain includes the laboratory; its samplers, analysts, scientific director and quality personnel; cultivators and other clients; the Nevada Department of Taxation, which then regulated the market; investigators; the hearing officer; the Cannabis Compliance Board; and the standards, accreditation and proficiency-testing bodies referenced in the case.

Nevada did not invent chromatography, microbial testing, certificates of analysis, ISO/IEC 17025 or laboratory quality systems. The Nevada revelation is institutional: the state assigned market consequences to laboratory outputs, discovered that the decision rules and reporting practices behind those outputs could be manipulated, and reconstructed the conduct through records and administrative adjudication. Any claim that this case itself caused a later Nevada reform remains unresolved until a dated rulemaking or implementation trail proves it.

### 3. Period + products/processes

The story requires a disciplined chronology rather than a single scandal date:

- 2017: prior inspection history and an earlier unsubstantiated allegation appear in the final-order record; neither proves nor excuses the later conduct.
- 2018–2019: relevant proficiency, quality and laboratory practices developed; the central retesting conduct examined in the case occurred in this period.
- 2019: investigators examined laboratory records, including microbial, cadmium and potency practices.
- 2020: public advisories and summary-suspension/correction proceedings occurred; these were not the final adjudication.
- 26 January 2021: the CCB filed its complaint.
- April–October 2021: the matter received twenty hearing days.
- 2022–2023: the Board considered motions, argument and violations.
- 4 March 2024: the corrected final order imposed $57,500 and a 180-day suspension.
- 12 August 2026 audit: the official business record lists LettuceTest LLC, license L006, as active. Recheck immediately before publication.

The relevant “products” are both cannabis lots and informational products: raw test data, invalid/failing/passing results, trend logs, state reports and COAs. The story should show how a result moves from observation to market classification.

### 4. Infrastructure + present-day ecosystem

The infrastructure is a coupled physical, software, documentary and human system: sample collection and custody; preparation; analytical and microbial methods; instruments and quality controls; LIMS or reporting software; spreadsheets and trend logs; SOPs; proficiency testing; accreditation; state data submission; Metrc where applicable; regulatory inspection; evidentiary exhibits; administrative hearings; and penalties.

The labor must be visible: samplers, sample-receiving staff, microbiology and chemistry analysts, the potency chemist, scientific leadership, QA staff, client personnel, data-entry workers, investigators, lawyers, the hearing officer and Board members. The final order names and differentiates people; the article must not flatten every employee into a participant in misconduct.

The present-day consequence is not “the bad lab was eliminated.” It is that Nevada still depends on independent laboratories and the trustworthiness of their hidden decision rules. The CCB listed eight active adult-use independent testing laboratories statewide in July 2026, while the respondent's current license record is active. The story's durable question is how a regulator and the public can verify the process behind a certificate—not whether a certificate should be abandoned.

## Six-field technology spine

1. **Central technology/system:** the end-to-end sample, retest, result-selection, reporting and certificate-control system that converts laboratory observations into a regulatory and market decision.
2. **Tangible technology/object/process/breakthrough:** physical samples and aliquots; sample-preparation procedures; microbial and chemistry testing; instrument quality controls; cadmium and cannabinoid results; SOPs; trend logs; retest permissions; emails; investigator spreadsheets; state reporting records; and actual COAs. The exact instruments and methods remain dossier questions and must not be invented.
3. **People and labor:** laboratory analysts, potency chemist, scientific director, QA workers, samplers, cultivator/client employees, investigators, regulators, lawyers, hearing officer and Board.
4. **Place-specific historical connection:** a Las Vegas laboratory serving Nevada licensees became a state-authorized measurement gate; Nevada investigators reconstructed its 2018–2019 practices and Nevada's CCB adjudicated the case through 2024.
5. **Present-day technological consequence/continuation:** active Nevada laboratories still produce market-governing results, and the respondent is again listed as active. Current rules, oversight, accreditation, data systems and retest controls must be checked to establish what has continued or changed.
6. **Tempting but merely concurrent or unresolved connections:** high-THC consumer demand is not, without case evidence, the cause of each retest; a possible price premium was not clearly established as universal; accreditation does not prove a result correct; a COA does not create scientific truth; an active license does not erase past findings; one laboratory's conduct does not show all Nevada laboratories are unreliable; later reforms are not descendants of this case unless rulemaking evidence proves causation.

## Adjudicated findings the package may use

Subject to quotation and context verification against the final PDF:

- Investigators identified 232 samples from three clients that initially failed microbial testing and were retested at least once during September–November 2019.
- The order describes instances in which samples failed twice and passed once but were reported as passing; the laboratory's stated majority-of-three approach was not followed consistently.
- Initial microbial failures and other retest results were not all reported, retesting occurred without required permission, and documented invalid results did not justify the retests.
- Chemistry samples were used for microbial tests in identified instances; the order found that the different handling and possible solvent exposure could create false-negative microbial results.
- The Board found unapproved cadmium retesting, incomplete reporting and selection of passing results despite failed results.
- For potency, the final order found unauthorized retesting, incomplete reporting and no defensible selection method. Trend logs showed selected reported results continuously increasing. The order found that the laboratory worked with clients to increase reported potency and used extensive retesting to control cannabinoid results and avoid reporting lower results.
- The Board found one Category I, three Category II and nine Category III violations; it also found no violation on several complaint paragraphs.
- Final discipline was a $57,500 total civil penalty and 180-day suspension. The order required the laboratory to cease operations and remove cannabis during that suspension; it did not revoke the license.
