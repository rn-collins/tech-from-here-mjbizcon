## Story 1 — Nevada Put Medical Cannabis in Its Constitution

**Gate:** DRAFTABLE. Legal update and cite-check required before release.

### Reader promise and thesis

Most states treated medical cannabis as a statutory program. Nevada voters placed a legislatively conditioned medical-cannabis command in the state constitution after approving the proposal in two consecutive general elections. The article explains what constitutionalization did, what it did not do, and why legal durability is not the same as practical access.

### Working hooks

- **Canonical:** Before Nevada built a dispensary system, its voters put medical cannabis in the state constitution.
- **LinkedIn:** Nevada did not begin its medical-cannabis story with a storefront. It began with two statewide votes and a constitutional instruction to the Legislature.
- **Carousel cover:** Nevada constitutionalized medical cannabis. It still had to build access.

### Narrative spine

1. **Cold open — the unexpected text.** Put Article 4, section 38 on the page as a physical/legal object. Establish that this is constitutional text, not a slogan.
2. **Why two elections.** Use the official 2000 ballot pamphlet to show that voters had first approved the amendment in 1998 and had to approve it again in 2000.
3. **What the provision commands.** Explain physician advice, specified or legislatively approved conditions, and the patient/attendant registry requirement.
4. **What it leaves to institutions.** The Legislature and administrative state still had to create definitions, registration, rules, and implementation.
5. **Counterweight.** A constitutional provision did not create inventory, a seller, an inspection regime, or a storefront.
6. **Closing turn.** Constitutionalization changed the legal foundation; Story 2 asks how long it took before that foundation supported a regulated supply system.

### Paragraph evidence map

| Movement | Claim permitted | Primary assignment | Qualification that must travel |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening | Nevada's current constitution contains a medical-cannabis provision. | Nevada Constitution, art. 4, § 38 | It directs legislative provision; it is not an unrestricted right to obtain or use cannabis anywhere. |
| Election mechanism | Question 9 was approved first in 1998 and again in 2000. | LCB, *Statewide Ballot Questions 2000*, Question 9 | Use official election language; do not invent voter motivations. |
| Operative scope | The provision addresses physician-advised use and a registry. | Nev. Const. art. 4, § 38(1)(a)–(c) | Preserve conditions and legislative role. |
| Limit | The amendment did not itself build retail supply. | Constitutional text read against later implementing chronology | This is a legal/institutional distinction, not proof of every patient's experience. |

### Counterevidence and barred claims

- Do not call the text an unrestricted individual right.
- Do not say constitutionalization legalized a retail market in 2000.
- Do not infer why individual Nevadans voted yes without voter research.
- Do not claim the amendment alone protected every patient from arrest or federal consequences.

### Visual proof and rights plan

- Hero: original photograph or rights-cleared scan treatment of the official ballot/constitution, with the relevant language legible.
- Evidence panel: 1998 → 2000 two-vote chronology built from official records.
- Backup accessible image: Nevada Legislative Building or official election material with source/rights record.
- Avoid decorative cannabis leaves; the visual proposition is constitutional design.

### Carousel logic

1. Nevada put medical cannabis in its constitution.
2. It took two statewide votes.
3. The text told the Legislature what to build.
4. It covered specified medical use and a registry.
5. It did not create a storefront.
6. Durable law and practical access are different systems.
7. Save this: constitutional recognition ≠ supply infrastructure.

### Release checklist

- Recheck current Article 4, section 38.
- Cite-check the two-election procedure and effective chronology.
- Complete image-rights record and alt text.
- Keep implementation details assigned to Story 2.

## Story 2 — The Constitutional Protection Without a Storefront

**Gate:** DRAFTABLE as an institutional chronology. Exact first-opening claims, statewide practical access, and patient experience remain reporting gates.

### Reader promise and thesis

Nevada separated constitutional recognition, patient registration, establishment authorization, application review, and actual storefront operation into different events spread across years. The story is not that patients had no legal access mechanism; it is that legal protection and a regulated commercial supply network were not the same thing.

### Working hooks

- **Canonical:** Nevada recognized medical cannabis years before it built the system that could sell it.
- **LinkedIn:** A right, a registry, a license, and an open door are four different things.
- **Carousel cover:** Nevada had constitutional protection before it had storefront access.

### Narrative spine

1. **Return to election night.** The constitutional command exists, but no dispensary appears when the votes are counted.
2. **2001 — registration and exemption.** Explain the patient/caregiver framework and cultivation/possession mechanisms without recasting them as a retail system.
3. **Administrative interlude.** Note the program's 2009 agency transfer as evidence that institutional ownership evolved before commercial establishments existed.
4. **2013 — establishment architecture.** SB 374 creates dispensaries, cultivation, production, and independent testing categories.
5. **2014 — applications are not access.** Separate application intake/review from provisional approval, local permission, inspection, inventory, and opening.
6. **2015 — operating phase.** Describe emergence of stores only at the level supported by official records; do not name a definitive statewide “first” without contemporaneous licensing proof.
7. **Human question.** Flag geography, patient counts, cultivation ability, disability, cost, and federal risk as practical-access variables requiring patient records/reporting.
8. **Closing turn.** Institutions can recognize a need long before they make it usable.

### Controlled chronology

| Date | Institutional event | Exact article use | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1998/2000 | Voters approve Question 9 twice. | Establish constitutional recognition. | Not retail supply. |
| 2001 | AB 453 creates registry/prosecution-exemption framework. | Show implementation begins. | Early cultivation/caregiver routes existed; do not write “no access of any kind.” |
| 2009 | Program moves from Agriculture to Health Division. | Show administrative evolution. | Agency transfer is not an access outcome. |
| 2013-06-11 | SB 374 becomes law. | Establish medical-establishment categories. | Enactment is not an open store. |
| 2014-08-05 phase | Establishment application review begins. | Show the licensing pipeline. | Application is not certification, inspection, inventory, or operation. |
| 2015 phase | Licensed operations emerge. | Mark the storefront phase cautiously. | One opening does not prove statewide access. |

### Sources assigned

- Nevada Constitution, art. 4, § 38.
- Nevada 2001 Session Laws / AB 453 implementation record.
- Official Nevada legislative marijuana timeline.
- 2013 SB 374 / chapter 547.
- Current NRS chapter 678C for legal updating.
- Official fiscal, state, and local licensing records for any named facility/opening.

### Counterevidence and barred claims

- Do not write that patients had a constitutional right to purchase cannabis in 2000.
- Do not erase home cultivation, caregiver, or possession mechanisms.
- Do not treat a first storefront as statewide practical access.
- Do not substitute later press retrospectives for contemporaneous licensing evidence.
- Patient experience must come from patients or contemporaneous records, not inferred from statutes.

### Visual proof and rights plan

- Primary: a layered timeline in which “recognition,” “registration,” “establishments,” “applications,” and “operation” are separate tracks.
- Real-life hero request: rights-cleared photograph of an early Nevada medical dispensary, patient card, or advocacy-era artifact with verified date/provenance.
- Backup: official session-law pages and a present-day exterior photographed by RN, clearly labeled as present-day rather than historical.

### Carousel logic

1. A constitutional amendment is not a supply chain.
2. 2000: constitutional command.
3. 2001: registry and legal framework.
4. 2013: establishment categories.
5. 2014: applications and review.
6. 2015: operating phase.
7. Recognition → registration → licensing → access.
8. The gap between law and use is where institutions become visible.

### Release checklist

- Retrieve first final certificates, local approvals, inspections, and opening records before naming any first facility.
- Add registered-patient/geographic evidence if making an access-distribution claim.
- Seek at least one contemporaneous patient perspective or keep experience explicitly open.
- Legal cite-check immediately before publication.

## Story 3 — Nevada Used the Shape of the Data to Police Potency

**Gate:** METHOD-BOUND. Draft only as an investigation into screening logic and missing data. Analytical-chemist and statistician review required.

### Reader promise and thesis

Nevada's regulator says it compared laboratory-result distributions, investigated suspected THC inflation, and used results above 30% as a review trigger. The revealing story is not “a histogram proved fraud.” It is how the shape of aggregate data became an enforcement lead—and how little the public chart can prove without denominators, methods, raw values, case links, and counterfactuals.

### Working hooks

- **Canonical:** Nevada looked at the shape of thousands of potency results and saw a reason to investigate.
- **LinkedIn:** A histogram can tell a regulator where to look. It cannot tell you who lied.
- **Carousel cover:** Can the shape of cannabis data expose inflated potency?

### Narrative spine

1. **Cold open — two histograms.** Invite the reader to notice the different shapes before explaining the regulator's interpretation.
2. **Why potency has incentives.** Briefly explain why reported THC can affect product positioning while avoiding unsupported Nevada price-causation claims.
3. **What the CCB says it did.** Agents reviewed practices across all Nevada laboratories; the report says suspected inflators were identified and later distributions resembled the earlier pattern.
4. **The >30% trigger.** Explain a review threshold as a screening device, not a biological maximum or automatic violation.
5. **What a histogram sees.** Distribution shape, clustering, heaping, tails, and change over time can generate questions.
6. **What it cannot see.** Product mix, sample handling, moisture/dry-weight conventions, rounding, duplicates, retests, laboratory identity, and causal mechanism are missing.
7. **The missing docket is a finding.** The public cannot reproduce the inference without bins, denominators, exclusions, protocols, and case outcomes.
8. **Closing turn.** Data can make regulation more observant without making it omniscient.

### Evidence assignment

| Claim | Primary assignment | Required wording |
|---|---|---|
| CCB investigated potency practices across all laboratories. | CCB, *Biennial Report 2025*, laboratory section | Attribute to CCB. |
| CCB says suspected inflators were identified and the practice was curbed. | Same report | “The regulator reports”; not independent causal proof. |
| >30% triggers review. | Same report | Trigger, not ceiling or violation. |
| Histograms changed between displayed periods. | Published figures | Describe only visible/published comparison; no invented bin values. |
| Distributional screening can generate leads. | Statistical explanation plus expert review | Do not identify a lab or sample without case evidence. |

### Missing-data box that must appear in the article

- Machine-readable bin counts and denominators.
- Date windows, exclusions, product-type controls, and laboratory universe.
- Total-THC formula, dry-weight convention, rounding, duplicates, and retests.
- Trigger workflow and investigative escalation.
- Named complaints, settlements, sanctions, or exonerations tied to the chart.
- Product-mix or other counterfactual explanations.

### Counterevidence and barred claims

- The charts do not independently prove fraud.
- A >30% result is not automatically false.
- The later distribution does not, by itself, prove enforcement caused the change.
- Do not name or imply a laboratory unless supported by a final primary record.
- Do not turn “20–25% general average” into a natural law.

### Visual proof and rights plan

- Hero: a faithful redraw of the official paired histograms, labeled as a visual reconstruction if underlying values are unavailable.
- Evidence annotation: “what the chart shows” versus “what the chart cannot establish.”
- Real-life image request: laboratory instrument/sample workflow photographed with permission; backup is the regulator's report/chart under documented reuse rules.
- Never fabricate chart precision by tracing approximate pixels into false exact values.

### Carousel logic

1. Nevada saw something in the shape of its THC data.
2. The regulator compared potency distributions.
3. Results above 30% triggered review.
4. A histogram can reveal anomalies.
5. It cannot identify intent or causation.
6. Missing: bins, denominators, methods, and case links.
7. Data can direct enforcement without proving the case.
8. Save this: screening evidence ≠ adjudicative proof.

### Release checklist

- Recapture report pages and exact captions.
- Seek underlying values/method and enforcement records.
- Obtain analytical-chemistry and statistics review.
- Use uncertainty-forward headline/copy throughout.

## Story 4 — Every Regulated Plant Acquires a Data Double

**Gate:** DRAFTABLE. Privacy/security and current-system-version review remain.

### Reader promise and thesis

In Nevada's regulated market, a plant does not remain only a plant. Tags, harvest weights, packages, production records, manifests, samples, COAs, sales, surveillance, adjustments, holds, and destruction records form a revisable data representation around it. “Data double” is the useful metaphor; perfect digital twin is the claim the evidence does not support.

### Working hooks

- **Canonical:** In Nevada, a regulated cannabis plant grows a second body made of data.
- **LinkedIn:** The physical plant can be harvested, packaged, tested, transferred, sold, held, recalled, or destroyed. Its data double has to survive every transformation.
- **Carousel cover:** Every regulated cannabis plant acquires a data double.

### Narrative spine

1. **Cold open — attach the tag.** A physical plant receives a regulated identifier.
2. **Growth and harvest.** Records represent status, plant/plant batch, harvest, timing, and weight.
3. **Transformation.** One source becomes packages and derivative products; lineage branches.
4. **Movement.** Manifests connect origin, destination, date, and quantity.
5. **Measurement.** Samples and COAs attach bounded laboratory knowledge to a lot or batch.
6. **Sale and visibility.** Point-of-sale and Item Catalog exposure create different public/regulator views.
7. **When reality and record diverge.** Adjustments, delayed entry, outages, sampling limits, surveillance blind spots, and human error make the representation revisable.
8. **Bridge to recall.** Holds and amended warnings show why a data double matters most when the product must be found backward and downstream.
9. **Closing turn.** Regulation increasingly governs not just cannabis, but the integrity of the relationship between an object and its records.

### Lifecycle evidence map

| Physical event | Data representation | Verification | Limit to state |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plant exists | Tag/identifier and facility record | Inventory/inspection | Not full biology. |
| Harvest occurs | Harvest/log record and weight | Reconciliation/log audit | Human measurement and entry. |
| Product is made | Package/item/production lineage | Production records | Descendant links can be entered incorrectly. |
| Transfer occurs | Manifest/origin/destination/quantity | Sending/receiving reconciliation | Manifest does not prove every transit fact. |
| Sample is tested | Sample, lab, result, COA | Chain of custody/raw-data review | Applies to sample and required analytes. |
| Sale occurs | Item/facility/sales record | POS reconciliation | Does not guarantee consumer identification. |
| Hazard/discrepancy appears | Adjustment, hold, recall, destruction/release | Regulator/facility evidence | Record is revisable, not omniscient. |

### Source assignments

- CCB Guidance/Metrc: required participation and reporting since 1 November 2017.
- Current NCCR: inventory, transfer, testing, sales, security, surveillance, and record requirements.
- CCB recall and administrative-hold records: system use under stress.
- Item Catalog materials: limited public-facing exposure; do not merge with regulator access.

### Counterevidence and barred claims

- Do not call the database a lossless, real-time replica.
- Do not say a tag proves compliance.
- Do not treat required reporting as proof every record is complete or timely.
- Surveillance is a second evidence layer, not total visibility.
- Do not expose operational security details beyond public records.

### Visual proof and rights plan

- Hero request: real tagged plant or package photographed at a licensed facility with permission and no sensitive identifiers exposed.
- Core visual: plant → harvest → package → transfer → test → sale → hold/recall lifecycle, with limits under each node.
- Backup: official Metrc/CCB public materials and label/package photography with verified provenance.
- Redact tag numbers, personal data, security placement, and proprietary screens unless expressly cleared.

### Carousel logic

1. The plant is physical. Its compliance life is digital.
2. Tag.
3. Harvest and weight.
4. Package and lineage.
5. Manifest and movement.
6. Sample and COA.
7. Sale, hold, recall, destruction.
8. The record is powerful—and revisable.
9. Save this: a data double represents reality; it does not replace proof.

### Release checklist

- Recheck current NCCR provisions and guidance versions.
- Verify correction/adjustment workflow with a public source or operator.
- Conduct privacy/security review of every screenshot or photograph.
- Ensure Story 15 owns the full recall case study to avoid duplication.

## Story 5 — Twelve Governments, Twelve Agreements

**Gate:** STRUCTURAL ONLY. Interpretive drafting and release are blocked pending page-image clause verification, operative-status confirmation, and nation-specific review.

### Reader promise and bounded structural thesis

Nevada's state–tribal cannabis agreements are not evidence that twelve sovereign governments became one system. They are cross-jurisdictional instruments that coordinate selected interfaces—tracking, testing, packaging, transport, commerce, fiscal/audit arrangements, inspection/cooperation, disputes, and termination—while retaining jurisdictional and sovereignty boundaries. Shared architecture is visible; equivalence, motive, benefit, harm, and community consensus are not established.

### Working hooks held for review

- **Canonical:** What happens when cannabis crosses not just a county line, but a jurisdictional boundary between governments?
- **LinkedIn:** Interoperability is not the same as surrendering sovereignty.
- **Carousel cover:** Twelve governments. Twelve agreements. No single “tribal cannabis” story.

These hooks are provisional and must be reviewed for framing before publication.

### Authorized structural sequence

1. **Define the problem, not the nations.** A regulated product may encounter different licensing and jurisdictional systems when commerce crosses boundaries.
2. **Inventory the documents.** On 10 August 2026, the CCB live page exposed twelve named agreements; list each nation/colony exactly.
3. **Show clause families.** Tracking, cross-border sale/transport, testing/packaging/labeling, fiscal/audit, inspection/cooperation, disputes/termination, and sovereignty language.
4. **Show differences.** Pyramid Lake's linked instrument is expressly medical; later agreements use different terminology and structures. Do not rank or generalize motives.
5. **Explain interoperability cautiously.** Defined data, product, compliance, and notice interfaces enable specified cross-border activity.
6. **Center the limit.** Contract text cannot establish a nation's priorities, lived outcomes, community views, or whether the agreement worked as intended.

### Current document inventory

Elko Band of the Te-Moak Tribe; Ely Shoshone Tribe; Fallon Paiute-Shoshone Tribe; Fort McDermitt Paiute and Shoshone Tribe; Las Vegas Paiute Tribe; Lovelock Paiute Tribe; Moapa Band of Paiute Indians; Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe; Reno-Sparks Indian Colony; Timbisha Shoshone Tribe; Walker River Paiute Tribe; and Winnemucca Indian Colony.

Yerington is not counted because it was not linked in the captured live inventory. The count and operative status require refresh.

### Required verification packet before prose

For every agreement, record:

- Exact title, parties, execution/effective dates, signatories, amendments, and current operative status.
- Medical/adult-use scope and defined enterprises.
- Licensing responsibility.
- Tracking/data exchange, retention, and protection.
- Testing, sampling, packaging, labeling, and laboratory rules.
- Transport, manifests, delivery verification, and authorized counterparties.
- Inspection, notice, investigation, hold, discipline, and enforcement cooperation.
- Tax, fees, remittance, audit, and fiscal treatment.
- Express sovereignty reservations, limited waivers, jurisdiction, dispute, termination, and survival.
- Page-image locator for every quotation; OCR may locate but never authenticate text.

### Interpretation prohibitions

- Never describe sovereignty as a loophole.
- Never describe state-system participation as surrender of sovereignty.
- Never merge twelve governments into a generic actor.
- Never infer motive, benefit, harm, consent, or community consensus from compact text.
- Never solicit or publish restricted cultural knowledge.
- Never treat a state-hosted PDF as cultural authority to narrate a nation's history.

2. Confirm current status with the state and the relevant nation.
3. Prepare a nation-specific summary containing only the proposed references to that nation.
4. Offer a meaningful factual/framing review opportunity; record response or nonresponse without implying endorsement.

### Visual proof and rights plan

- No flags, seals, people, lands, cultural objects, or community images as decoration.
- Any agreement-page reproduction requires rights/use review and sufficient context.

### Carousel architecture held for review

4. Tracking is not jurisdiction.

## Story 6 — The Public Item Catalog Is a Window, Not the Whole Market

**Gate:** AUDIT-BOUND. The official scope and audit method are draftable; findings require a completed dated sample.

### Reader promise and thesis

Nevada gives the public an unusual window into regulated products and downloadable certificates of analysis through a Metrc-linked Item Catalog. But the interface is a search-time snapshot of current availability, limited to items sold in the preceding six months. Transparency is therefore not the mere existence of a portal; it is whether real people can find, open, understand, and correctly bound the evidence it provides.

### Working hooks

- **Canonical:** Nevada lets consumers look behind the label. The view has edges.
- **LinkedIn:** A downloadable lab report is not the same thing as understandable evidence.
- **Carousel cover:** Nevada opened a window into cannabis testing. How much can you actually see?

### Narrative spine

1. **Cold open — scan/search a product.** Follow a real dated query from product/item to available COA.
2. **What the state says the tool is.** Current products at licensed dispensaries/lounges, Metrc-derived, with product/testing information.
3. **The time boundary.** Six-month sales window and separate route for older laboratory information.
4. **The reproducibility test.** Explain the sample before reporting results.
5. **Coverage findings.** Only after audit: which item types, laboratories, jurisdictions, and records are present or missing.
6. **Usability findings.** Mobile/desktop, search terms, download behavior, accessibility, and explanation.
7. **Scientific-legibility findings.** Whether users can identify batch, lab, test date, analytes, results, limits, and qualifications without mistaking “pass” for universal safety.
8. **Closing turn.** Public data becomes transparency only when its scope, omissions, and meaning are legible.

### Official facts draftable now

- The CCB publicly provides the Nevada Cannabis Metrc Item Catalog.
- It searches products currently available at licensed Nevada dispensaries and consumption lounges.
- It exposes product/testing information and downloadable COAs.
- It reflects Metrc at the time of search.
- It displays items sold within the preceding six months; older testing data follows a different route.

### Reproducible audit protocol

Sample flower, pre-roll, concentrate, edible, beverage, topical, and lounge/dispensary items across multiple manufacturers, laboratories, and jurisdictions. Log for every attempt:

- Query text and timestamp.
- Device/browser and mobile/desktop state.
- Item identifier, seller, product type, manufacturer, and jurisdiction.
- Laboratory and test date.
- COA presence, download success, and file integrity.
- Fields displayed and fields missing.
- Label-to-catalog and label-to-COA match.
- Keyboard, screen-reader, contrast, zoom, and plain-language support.
- Whether analytes, units, action limits, qualifiers, LOD/LOQ, and pass/fail meaning are legible.

### Counterevidence and barred claims

- Do not call the catalog complete, historical, accurate, accessible, or consumer-friendly before testing.
- Do not infer universal COA coverage from one successful query.
- Do not say a downloadable COA is self-explanatory.
- Do not treat six months as the product's full regulatory history.
- A dated interface capture must not be presented as permanent behavior.

### Visual proof and rights plan

- Hero: RN's own dated screen capture of a successful search with sensitive/proprietary details reviewed.
- Audit visual: sample flow and results table, including failures and missingness rather than only best cases.
- Real-life pairing: photograph the physical product label with permission/ownership, then show the matching catalog/COA trail.
- Backup: interface architecture diagram derived from official descriptions if product imagery is unavailable.

### Carousel logic

1. Nevada gives the public product-level testing access.
2. Search a current item.
3. Open the COA.
4. The view reflects Metrc at search time.
5. The sales window is six months.
6. Availability is not completeness.
7. Downloadability is not scientific legibility.
8. Our audit asks: find it, open it, match it, understand it.
9. Save this: a public portal is a claim that should be tested.

### Release checklist

- Run and preserve the dated, cross-category audit.
- Repeat critical failures before characterizing them.
- Perform accessibility and scientific-legibility review.
- Recapture the interface within 30 days of publication.
- Preserve screenshots, query log, COAs, and rights/provenance record.

## Story 7 — Borrowed Architecture, Different Machine

**Gate:** DRAFTABLE. Provision-level legal crosswalk and prepublication legal review required.

### Reader promise and thesis

Nevada did not merely compare cannabis to gaming as a rhetorical flourish. The enacted AB 533 digest and legislative testimony expressly describe the Cannabis Compliance Board as generally modeled on, or intended to mimic aspects of, Nevada's gaming regulator. The article identifies the borrowed institutional architecture while showing why a biological consumer product in a federally illegal market is a fundamentally different regulatory machine.

### Working hooks

- **Canonical:** When Nevada needed a cannabis regulator, it reached for the institutional machinery it knew best.
- **LinkedIn:** Nevada did not copy gaming regulation onto a plant. But it deliberately borrowed parts of the architecture.
- **Carousel cover:** Nevada modeled cannabis oversight on gaming—then the analogy broke.

### Narrative spine

1. **Cold open — the phrase in the law.** Start with the enacted digest's “generally modeled after” language.
2. **Why the comparison matters.** Nevada had an existing vocabulary of boards, commissions, suitability, investigation, centralized expertise, and regulated-industry integrity.
3. **Board architecture.** Compare appointment, membership, expertise, terms, and institutional relationships provision by provision.
4. **Suitability and ownership.** Show how cannabis licensing investigates people, ownership, finance, and eligibility without declaring the standards identical.
5. **Procedure and rulemaking.** Examine the relationship to ordinary Nevada administrative procedure and what transparency/due-process questions that raises.
6. **Enforcement and conditionality.** Compare investigation, conditional/final approval, discipline, and appeals using actual authority.
7. **Where the analogy breaks.** Cannabis is ingested/inhaled, biologically variable, laboratory tested, recalled, federally prohibited, and entwined with medical/patient and social-equity history.
8. **Closing turn.** Institutional borrowing is a design choice: it brings capabilities, assumptions, and blind spots from the system being borrowed.

### Crosswalk required in prose

| Dimension | Cannabis evidence | Comparison boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Creation | AB 533 created the CCB and Cannabis Advisory Commission and transferred authority. | Creation date/transfer must follow enacted sections. |
| Explicit lineage | Enacted digest and legislative testimony invoke the gaming model. | “Modeled on” is not “identical to.” |
| Board structure | Five-member appointed board and expertise categories. | Compare exact gaming provisions before stating similarity. |
| Suitability | Cannabis licensing/ownership review. | Similar word does not prove same standard, burden, or outcome. |
| Procedure | Statutory relationship to NRS chapter 233B. | Explain consequences; do not assume efficiency or unfairness. |
| Enforcement | Investigation, discipline, conditional/final licensing. | Compare authority and appeal routes, not aesthetics. |
| Regulatory object | Cannabis product, laboratory chain, public health, federal conflict. | Gaming and cannabis risks are not interchangeable. |

### Primary assignments

- Enrolled AB 533 and 2019 Nevada Statutes: creation, structure, transfer, and “generally modeled after” statement.
- 2019 Senate Judiciary minutes: contemporary design testimony.
- CCB SB 49 testimony: intent to mimic gaming-regulator centralization.
- 2023 Senate Commerce and Labor minutes: procedural/NRS 233B comparison.
- Current cannabis and gaming statutes: final provision-level crosswalk.

### Counterevidence and barred claims

- Do not say Nevada copied the entire Gaming Control Board.
- Similar vocabulary alone does not prove institutional causation; use direct statements and provisions.
- Do not romanticize gaming oversight as uniquely effective without outcome evidence.
- Do not infer that a gaming-derived structure is necessarily suitable or unsuitable for cannabis.
- Distinguish Board, Commission, executive, agency, and local powers accurately.

### Visual proof and rights plan

- Hero: original photograph pairing the Nevada regulatory landscape—e.g., official public buildings or public meeting setting—not casino spectacle plus cannabis decoration.
