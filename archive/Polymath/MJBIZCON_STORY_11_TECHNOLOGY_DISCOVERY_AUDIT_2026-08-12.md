# MJBizCon Story 11 — Technology-Discovery Audit

**Audit date:** 12 August 2026  
**Prior working title:** *Legalization Entered the Archive: The Vicki Higgins Cannabis Advocacy Papers*  
**Decision:** **CURRENT ARCHIVE/ADVOCACY PROPOSITION FAILS. SLOT PASSES PROVISIONALLY ONLY AS A FUNDAMENTAL REDESIGN ABOUT THE TECHNOLOGY AND LABOR THAT MADE A PRIVATE CANNABIS RECORD PUBLICLY ADDRESSABLE. REPRESENTATIVE BOX SAMPLING AND COMPARATIVE SELECTION REMAIN HARD GATES.**

## Controlling correction

The existence of an archive is not itself enough for *Tech From Here*. Nor may visually rich pamphlets, correspondence, publications and ephemera substitute for a technical mechanism.

The broad discovery pass does, however, identify a potentially distinct system. In 2026, Vicki Higgins donated nine boxes—5.83 cubic feet—of approximately 2000–2020 Nevada cannabis advocacy material to UNLV Special Collections and Archives. UNLV records that Landon Paljusaj created the accession record in ArchivesSpace; Sarah Jones sorted and rehoused the material and created its ArchivesSpace description. The institution retained the original gift, preserved much of the received arrangement, grouped other material by year, separated certain publications into individually described catalog records, applied DACS description, assigned an EAD identifier and exposed the collection through a searchable portal, PDF finding aid and persistent ARK link.

The possible technology story is therefore not “legalization entered an archive.” It is how archival labor and information infrastructure transform privately accumulated, physically fragile and unevenly arranged records into a findable, citable and rights-bounded public evidence system.

## Provisional redesign proposition

**Nine Boxes of Nevada Cannabis History Became a Searchable Public Record.**

Alternate: **How Archivists Made Nevada's Cannabis Advocacy Addressable.**

This proposition survives only if representative box sampling demonstrates meaningful correspondence between the physical collection, its description and the history readers can recover—and if the archival mechanism beats stronger Canonical 14 replacement candidates.

## Required broad discovery pass

### 1. Place + technology

The place is Las Vegas across two linked environments: the advocacy networks, government meetings, conventions, publications and patient organizations in which the records were made; and UNLV's Lied Library, where those records were accessioned, rehoused, described and made discoverable.

The physical technology includes boxes, folders, labels, paper, publications, business cards, membership cards, convention catalogs and archival housings. The information technology includes ArchivesSpace, the finding-aid hierarchy, DACS description, EAD identification, the searchable Special Collections portal, catalog separation, PDF output and a persistent ARK identifier. These layers do not digitize every item or make the collection remotely readable; they create an address and map for records that generally still require reading-room access.

### 2. Institution + inventions

UNLV's documented workflow includes acquisition and accessioning, appraisal, arrangement, preservation/rehousing, description, rights/access notes, discovery and citation. The collection page names the labor: Visual Materials Curator Aaron Mayes acquired it; Paljusaj created the accession record; Jones sorted, rehoused and described it. The finding aid records that all material in the original gift was retained, one donor-foldered box remained labeled and other material was grouped by year.

ArchivesSpace, DACS, EAD and ARK are not UNLV inventions and cannot be presented as Nevada cannabis technologies. Their significance is institutional deployment: together they make a specific local historical record persistently identifiable and searchable. The article must explain what each layer does, what remains physical and invisible, and how archivists' descriptive choices shape discovery without claiming that the finding aid neutrally reproduces the past.

### 3. Period + products/processes

- **Approximately 2000–2020:** records accumulate through advocacy, research, legislation, news, organizations, conventions and publications.
- **2008–2019:** WECAN and related patient-rights work contribute organizational and communications records; the finding aid identifies media contacts, a handbook, symposium programs, local/state government material and ephemera.
- **2026:** Higgins donates the material; UNLV assigns accession 2026-040.
- **2026 processing:** an accession record is created, material is sorted and rehoused, description is entered in ArchivesSpace and a finding aid is published on 18 May 2026.
- **Present access:** researchers can search/browse the collection-level and component descriptions online, cite a persistent ARK and request physical research access. Publication still requires item-level copyright, trademark, privacy and publicity analysis.

The article should reconstruct one record's path from original use to box/folder context to description to researcher retrieval. Without that end-to-end case, it remains a generic explainer about archives.

### 4. Infrastructure + present-day ecosystem

The ecosystem includes Higgins and other record creators; WECAN and Coalition for Patient Rights Nevada participants; patients and people named in correspondence; journalists, lawmakers and businesses; UNLV curators, accessioning/processing archivists, catalogers, digital-platform staff and reference staff; ArchivesSpace and portal maintainers; standards communities; researchers and rights holders.

The public consequence is selective visibility. Searchable descriptions can surface a collection that was previously private, but descriptions are not item-level full text, access is not publication permission, retained material is not the whole legalization movement and donor accumulation is not representative sampling. Arrangement, description, separation, digitization choices, rights constraints and researcher ability all shape what becomes discoverable.

## Six-field technology spine

1. **Central technology or technological system:** the archival addressability stack that converts physical advocacy records into an accessioned, arranged, preserved, hierarchically described, searchable, persistently identified and citable collection.
2. **Tangible technology, object, process or breakthrough:** one verified item and its physical folder/box; archival enclosure and label; accession record; ArchivesSpace component record; DACS-compliant description; EAD identifier `US::NvLN::MS01235`; ARK `ark:/62930/f1cv38`; finding-aid PDF; catalog record for a separated publication. At least one end-to-end correspondence must be documented.
3. **People and labor:** record creators and subjects; Higgins as collector/donor; Aaron Mayes, Landon Paljusaj and Sarah Jones in their documented roles; cataloging, platform, preservation, reference and rights staff; researchers and rights holders. Do not make software the hero while erasing archival labor.
4. **Place-specific historical connection:** a Nevada cannabis-advocacy record made in Las Vegas and statewide institutions was donated to and processed by UNLV in 2026, becoming part of the local memory infrastructure through which future histories will be written.
5. **Present-day consequence or continuation:** the collection is now open for research, searchable at the descriptive level and persistently citable, while most items remain physically mediated and publication rights remain unresolved. The related Stephanie Fisher collection shows a broader UNLV cannabis-collecting context but not causal lineage.
6. **Tempting concurrent or noncausal connections:** digitization, OCR, web search, social media advocacy, cannabis databases and born-digital preservation are contemporary archival technologies, but the finding aid does not establish that this collection uses all of them. A portal record does not mean item digitization. The archive did not cause legalization; collecting records after events does not prove that every preserved communication caused policy change.

## Current story disposition

Retire *Legalization Entered the Archive* as the canonical proposition. Preserve it as the historical frame. The redesigned article must begin with a specific physical record and follow its transformation into an addressable archival component, then expose the limits: what the system describes, what it leaves in the box and what rights prevent from traveling onto the page.

## Mandatory representative sampling gate

No interpretive drafting may begin until RN or the production team, under reading-room rules, samples every major descriptive cluster rather than selecting only photogenic items. At minimum:

1. Boxes 1–4 annual advocacy/legislative files.
2. Boxes 4–6 city/state government and nonprofit records.
3. Box 6 WECAN programs, media contacts, business license, potential grow locations and Heller letter.
4. Box 7 handbook, catalogs, coalition records, correspondence and patient-rights files.
5. Boxes 8–9 ephemera, business cards, *Nevada Cannabis News*, membership objects and local press.
6. Separated publications and their catalog records.

For every sample, record box/folder title, item type, creator/date, original function, condition, descriptive correspondence, names/privacy, evidentiary value, silence/counterevidence, quotation limit, copyright owner, reproduction route and whether the item actually documents a technology.

If this sampling reveals a stronger Nevada-specific communications, publishing, patient-information, organizing or cultivation technology, compare it against the archival-addressability proposition rather than forcing it in. If neither mechanism survives comparison, remove Story 11.

## Comparative-selection and nonduplication gates

The story survives only if it demonstrates more than “archives use databases.” It must show why this particular 2026 Nevada cannabis collection makes archival infrastructure visible, including a verified item-to-description correspondence, named labor, meaningful failure modes and a public consequence.

- Story 11 owns historical-memory infrastructure, not the substantive technologies merely mentioned in archived publications.
- Story 6 owns the live public cannabis product interface.
- Story 2 owns patient identity/credentialing.
- Story 4 owns plant/package traceability.
- Story 7 owns regulatory observation if retained.
- Any technology discovered inside the boxes must compete as a new proposition and cannot be assumed to descend from the archive.
