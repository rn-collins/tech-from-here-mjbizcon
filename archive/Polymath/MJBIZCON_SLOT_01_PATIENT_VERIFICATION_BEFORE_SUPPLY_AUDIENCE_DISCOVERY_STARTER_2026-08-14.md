## Controlling decision

This pass validates the proposition's human intelligibility but does not clear the audience gate. Nevada patient and caregiver speech does support the central tension: the state could recognize a person as an authorized patient while leaving that person to grow cannabis, obtain it through legally precarious channels or go without. Later patient speech also makes the arrival of labeled, staffed retail supply emotionally legible.

The corpus is below the required fifteen quotations, almost entirely mediated through journalism, and mostly older than twelve months because the proposition concerns the 2001–2015 supply gap. It cannot establish how frequent any experience was, the exact historical registry workflow, the legality of a particular acquisition, or present-day audience demand. Do not pad the count with generic contemporary card questions, business-owner commentary, advocacy copy or statements from other states.

All language below is reproduced as published. These are evidence of individual expression, not proof of the underlying medical, legal or historical claim. Every item is older than twelve months and is necessarily flagged as historical.

## Questions the audience evidence actually raises

These are faithful analytical restatements of recovered concerns, not verbatim audience questions.

### Beginner

- What did a Nevada medical-cannabis card actually authorize before dispensaries opened?
- If a clinician recommended cannabis, where could a patient lawfully obtain a plant or seed?

### Troubleshooting

- What happened if illness, housing, disability or lack of horticultural skill made home cultivation unrealistic?
- How did a patient find help without exposing themself to stigma or enforcement?

### Comparison

- How did state verification differ from actual access?
- What changed when supply became licensed, labeled and staffed?

### Advanced

- Which agencies created, issued and verified the card, and what information traveled among them?
- Did the registry protect patients, make them administratively visible, or both?

## What was found versus what is inferred

### Found

- Nevada patients described difficulty obtaining cannabis despite holding a state card.
- Home cultivation was a real workaround for at least one documented patient and was described as unrealistic for some sick patients.
- A home grower described fear of arrest despite state authorization.
- A first-day customer valued cleanliness, labels and knowledgeable staff when licensed supply arrived.

### Inferred and requiring testing

- The strongest general-public hook may be **“the state could identify you as a patient before it could build somewhere for you to obtain the medicine.”**
- Disability and cultivation capacity may be the human center of the story.
- The card may function narratively as both protection and administrative visibility.
- A present-day audience may find the system-design contradiction more compelling than another legalization chronology.

None of those inferences establishes legal effect, prevalence, causation or present audience demand.
