# MJBizCon Slot 13 — Illicit-Market Estimation Technology-Discovery Audit

**Audit date:** 12 August 2026  
**Inherited proposition:** *Nevada Estimates an Illicit Cannabis Market Its Tracking System Cannot See*  
**Decision:** **PASS ONLY PROVISIONALLY AS A DISTINCT TECHNOLOGY-OF-KNOWLEDGE STORY. REVISE THE PROPOSITION. THE QUOTED DOLLAR RANGE IS NOT A DIRECT MEASUREMENT OR ONE NEVADA MODEL, AND DRAFTING IS BLOCKED UNTIL THE EXTERNAL METHODS AND UNCERTAINTY CAN BE RECONSTRUCTED.**

## Controlling correction

Nevada's 2024 *Cannabis Market Study*, commissioned by the Cannabis Compliance Board and produced by TPMA, does not observe illicit transactions through Metrc and does not generate one unified Nevada estimate. It explicitly calls estimation “an imprecise science at best” and presents rough indications from three different sources:

1. **CCB-distributed consumer survey + NSDUH prevalence + Census population:** the report combines a 29.54% past-year-use estimate with Nevada's adult population, then applies a reported 14–16% illicit-source share from the local survey to estimate approximately 102,000–119,000 Nevada adult illicit-source users per year. This is a user-count estimate, not the $242–$370 million sales range. The report itself warns that the consumer survey had few responses.
2. **Equio by New Frontier Data:** a commercial market projection estimated the 2023 illicit market at **$242 million**, about 21% of Nevada's total cannabis market, and separately estimated **$313 million for 2022**.
3. **Regulatory Determinants of Cannabis Outcomes Survey (RDCOS):** a June 2023 national quarterly cross-sectional survey estimated approximately 30% of cannabis purchased in Nevada was illicit. Applying that percentage to 2022 sales produced the report's **$370 million** estimate.

Therefore, the familiar “$242 million to $370 million” figure is a span between outputs built from different sources, years and methods—not a statistical confidence interval and not a volume of transactions counted by Nevada. The study's appendix also reports conflicting local-survey completion figures—114 completed responses in the limitations section and 166 Nevada consumer responses in the market-perspectives section—which must be resolved before any use.

## Revised proposition

**Nevada Put a $128 Million Gap Around a Cannabis Market It Cannot Directly See.**

Safer alternate: **How Nevada Turned Surveys and Models Into a Dollar Range for an Invisible Cannabis Market.**

“Gap” means the arithmetic difference between $242 million and $370 million; it is not an error bar. The article must disclose that the endpoints arise from different estimation systems. Retain Metrc only as the contrast: it records the licensed supply chain, while illicit-market size must be inferred from sources outside it.

## Required broad discovery pass

### 1. Place + technology

Nevada is unusually difficult to model as a resident-only cannabis market because Las Vegas joins a large visitor economy to uneven local access, jurisdictional prohibitions and a licensed market concentrated in Clark County. A statewide adult-population denominator does not automatically capture visitor purchasing; a Nevada sales total does not separate residents from tourists; seizure data reflect enforcement and detection; a voluntary survey distributed through regulator and industry channels may overrepresent people already connected to the licensed system.

The place-specific story is therefore about how Nevada translates a hidden, mobile and partly visitor-driven market into statewide quantities. It must test Clark County/Las Vegas, Washoe County and rural Nevada separately where evidence permits. “The Strip,” Las Vegas Boulevard, unlicensed smoke shops, home cultivation, social-media sellers, cross-border supply and sovereign cannabis establishments are not interchangeable categories and may sit under different legal and data regimes.

### 2. Institution + inventions

The estimation stack combines:

- Metrc and CCB/taxable-sales records for the observable licensed-market boundary;
- CCB survey design, electronic distribution and self-reported source behavior;
- NSDUH survey estimates of past-year cannabis use;
- Census population estimates;
- Equio/New Frontier Data commercial market projections;
- RDCOS quarterly cross-sectional survey estimates;
- arithmetic conversion from prevalence/share assumptions into users or dollars;
- DEA eradication counts as a partial enforcement indicator;
- charts, forecasts and report prose that convert heterogeneous inputs into a policy object.

The invention is not a magical illicit-market sensor. It is **data fusion under non-observation**: analysts define the hidden category, select proxies and denominators, normalize unlike sources, apply assumptions, produce scenarios, and communicate uncertainty to regulators and legislators.

### 3. Period + products/processes

- Nevada legalized medical cannabis constitutionally in 2000/2001 and opened licensed medical dispensaries in 2015.
- Voters approved adult use in 2016; legal adult-use sales began in 2017.
- Metrc made licensed products increasingly visible to the state, but by design cannot enumerate transactions that never enter licensed custody.
- The CCB commissioned TPMA's study against falling post-2021 licensed sales, changing license counts, access disparities and stakeholder concern about illicit competition.
- The September 2024 report used licensed data largely through September 2023, 2021–2022 NSDUH prevalence, 2022 population/sales, a June 2023 RDCOS estimate and 2023 Equio projections.
- By fiscal 2025, reported legal taxable sales had fallen to approximately $757.7 million. That later decline does not prove illicit growth or validate the 2024 model; tourism, price compression, consumption, product mix, interstate conditions and other factors could affect dollar sales.
- Nevada's 2026 “buy legal” campaign and Item Catalog show estimates becoming policy and communication inputs. Their appearance after the study does not prove the study caused them without decision records.

The canonical workflow is: define legal/illicit categories → establish observable legal sales → estimate total users or spending from surveys/models → infer the unobserved remainder or reported illicit share → construct scenarios/range → translate into tax/access/enforcement policy → update and validate against later evidence.

### 4. Infrastructure + present-day ecosystem

The people and systems include CCB commissioners and staff; TPMA researchers; commercial data/model vendors; survey designers, recruiters and respondents; SAMHSA/NSDUH and Census personnel; Department of Taxation analysts; licensed operators and workers; local governments; public-health researchers; economists/statisticians; law enforcement; journalists; consumers and people participating in criminalized or unlicensed markets.

The estimate can influence licensing, taxes, enforcement, consumer messaging, school-revenue narratives and public ideas about legalization's success. Those effects make model governance culturally and publicly relevant: who is sampled, what counts as “illicit,” whether tourists and homegrown cannabis are represented, and how uncertainty survives the trip from technical report to headline all matter.

## Six-field technology spine

1. **Central technology or technological system:** a multi-source market-estimation system that infers hidden cannabis use/spending outside the licensed tracking perimeter through surveys, population denominators, proprietary projections and scenario arithmetic.
2. **Tangible technology, object, process or breakthrough:** reconstruct the three estimate paths in a reproducible worksheet/data-flow map using the actual inputs, years, formulas, denominators and outputs; the $242 million and $370 million endpoints must remain visibly separate.
3. **People and labor:** survey respondents and recruiters; TPMA analysts; Equio/New Frontier and RDCOS/CPPC modelers; NSDUH, Census, tax and CCB data workers; enforcement personnel producing partial detection data; policymakers, journalists and advocates who translate the estimates; licensed and unlicensed market participants affected by resulting policy.
4. **Place-specific historical connection:** Nevada built a licensed tracking/tax system after medical and adult-use legalization, yet Las Vegas tourism, local opt-outs, uneven retail access and cross-jurisdictional movement create demand the licensed ledger cannot alone characterize.
5. **Present-day consequence or continuation:** the official study's estimates circulate in legislative testimony, revenue debates, enforcement claims and “buy legal” messaging, even though the underlying market remains unobserved and the principal endpoints use different methods and reference years.
6. **Tempting concurrent or noncausal connections:** falling legal dollar sales do not prove rising illicit volume; seizures do not measure prevalence; a larger modeled illicit share does not prove enforcement failure; taxes/access may influence purchasing but the Nevada study does not establish each as the cause of the estimated market; Metrc's blindness outside licensure is a design boundary, not a system malfunction; the 2026 campaign is not proven to result from the 2024 study; smoke-shop intoxicating-hemp sales, counterfeit cannabis, homegrown cannabis and prohibited sales cannot be merged without definitions.

## Comparative-selection judgment

Slot 13 survives only provisionally. Its strength is not the illicit market alone; it is the public technology of making an unseen market numerically governable. The $128 million disagreement is visually and intellectually strong, and it corrects regulatory-data triumphalism elsewhere in the slate.

Its weakness is substantial: the most important commercial model is opaque in the public report, the endpoints are not methodologically commensurate, the CCB survey is small and apparently carries inconsistent response counts, and a model story can become bloodless without people affected by its classifications. If Equio and RDCOS methods cannot be reconstructed sufficiently for independent statistical review, or if the story cannot secure human/place evidence without criminalizing or exposing vulnerable sources, demote Slot 13 and promote the ID-scanner or child-resistant-package alternate after its own audit.
