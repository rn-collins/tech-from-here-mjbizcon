## Why the audience search must use two vocabularies

Nevada’s May 2026 guidance creates regulatory categories and a processing workflow for cannabis flower combined with additional cannabis material. Consumers and sellers do not necessarily use those regulatory names. The public content market instead uses overlapping terms such as **moon rocks, sun rocks, caviar, diamond-infused flower, coated flower, infused nuggets, infused pre-rolls, THCA-infused flower** and brand-specific names.

The research therefore needs two linked but non-equivalent clusters:

1. **Nevada regulatory cluster:** the actual four categories, processing job, usable-cannabis classification, testing, labeling and traceability requirements.
2. **Audience-language cluster:** the names people encounter on menus, packages, product pages, videos and discussions.

Synonyms may be clustered for density analysis, but public quotations must retain the speaker’s own term. The article cannot assume that every “moon rock,” “diamond-infused” or “infused flower” product has the same construction.

## Initial finding: high content supply, insufficient primary voice

The bounded opening search did **not** produce a qualifying public quotation corpus. It returned:

- current commercial explainers defining moon rocks as flower plus concentrate plus kief;
- product and dispensary pages advertising diamond-infused flower;
- multiple unverified potency ranges;
- current Weedmaps explanatory content comparing moon rocks and sun rocks;
- rehabilitation/treatment content about high-potency products;
- irrelevant “infused” results about homemade beverages and butter;
- general vaping and concentrate discussions not about the selected product category;
- product pages with zero consumer reviews.

No vendor phrase, FAQ, product description, secondary claim, undated snippet or irrelevant consumer statement has been counted as voice of customer. **Current qualifying quotation count: 0/15 minimum.**

## Secondary content signals — not audience quotations

Commercial and explanatory pages repeatedly frame the product through:

- layered construction: flower plus concentrate and/or kief or crystalline material;
- extreme potency and “stronger than flower” positioning;
- visual novelty and luxury language;
- uncertainty about whether a grinder, joint, pipe, bong or vaporizer is suitable;
- inconsistent category boundaries among moon rocks, sun rocks, caviar and diamond-infused flower;
- claims that infusion preserves or enhances flavor;
- occasional concern that lower-quality flower may be hidden beneath coatings;
- product pages reporting very high THC values but sometimes showing no consumer reviews.

These patterns demonstrate saturation and likely questions. They do not establish product composition, safety, effects, prevalence, consumer belief or Nevada compliance.

## Public-curiosity hypotheses to test—not findings yet

1. What exactly was added to the flower?
2. Is this still “flower,” a concentrate, or a new category?
3. How strong is it compared with ordinary flower?
4. Does coating obscure the quality or condition of the underlying bud?
5. How should it be broken apart or consumed?
6. Does the package clearly distinguish loose infused flower from an infused pre-roll?
7. How can one batch be sampled and tested when added material may not be distributed evenly?
8. Are names like moon rocks, sun rocks, caviar and diamonds meaningful categories or marketing language?
9. Why did Nevada need four categories and a processing job now?
10. What changes on the label, COA, inventory record and public product catalog?

## Editorial opportunity hypothesis

This may be a particularly attractive Tech From Here story because the technology is physical and visible: a familiar agricultural object becomes a layered manufactured product, forcing classification, processing, sampling, testing, labeling and tracking systems to catch up. It connects consumer culture, material engineering, laboratory measurement, regulation, language and labor without requiring a generic policy frame.

That hypothesis is not yet audience-validated. The marketing-content abundance could indicate public interest, search optimization, seller demand creation, or all three. Primary voices are required before an opportunity score is assigned.
