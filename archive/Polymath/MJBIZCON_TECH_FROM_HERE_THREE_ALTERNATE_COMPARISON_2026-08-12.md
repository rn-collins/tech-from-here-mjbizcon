## Candidate 1 — Kief-enhanced usable cannabis

### What the Nevada record establishes

- Naturally occurring kief can separate incidentally during trimming, curing, packaging, transport, or ordinary handling.
- Intentionally separated kief results from a deliberate separation or concentration process, such as dry sifting, mechanical tumbling, ice-water extraction, or deliberate collection.
- Intentional separation is production/manufacturing activity requiring the appropriate license.
- Adding kief to usable cannabis does not make the finished product an infused cannabis product under the guidance.
- Effective June 1, 2026, Nevada created specific Metrc categories for kief-enhanced bulk flower, packaged flower, and count- or weight-based pre-rolls.
- The product must be created through a “Kief-Enhanced” processing job rather than a packaging activity or inventory adjustment.
- The job records kief weight, cannabis weight, and combined final weight; source and resulting lots must remain traceable.
- Kief must be tested before enhancement, the finished lot must be presented in full for random representative sampling, and the kief must be distributed homogeneously.
- The guidance says these controls do not add a new laboratory test panel; they change identification, traceability, and sampling discipline.

### Claims not yet supported

- That Nevada invented kief-enhanced flower or is the first state to create such categories.
- That every kief-enhanced product previously concealed enhancement intentionally.
- That a homogeneous-looking coating guarantees a homogeneous cannabinoid distribution.
- That a passing sample proves the entire lot is uniform.
- That all products marketed as “infused flower,” “diamond dusted,” “moon rocks,” or similar terms map to this category.
- That Nevada’s guidance resolves all product-category ambiguity outside the precise covered products.

## Candidate 2 — Tincture versus edible classification

### Corrected promise

> **Nevada Does Not Test the Bottle. It Tests the Route.**

The same broad product label—“tincture”—does not determine a single test category. Nevada’s 2025 guidance assigns testing by intended administration: oral ingestion as an edible with full homogeneity testing; topical application as a topical; and traditional sublingual or mucosal use as a concentrate or, after qualifying intermediate concentrate testing, an infused non-edible.

### Why it does not win

The classification turn is clean and useful, but the downstream mechanisms duplicate two recommended stories: edible homogeneity and dose-measuring packaging. A standalone story would need a stronger investigation into whether identical or near-identical formulations are sold with different intended-use instructions and testing histories. The guidance alone establishes the classification rule, not a consequential implementation failure.

## Candidate 3 — Terpene reintroduction

### Corrected promise

> **“Natural Flavor” Becomes a Different Question When You Breathe It.**

Nevada’s 2026 guidance treats terpene addition to inhalable products as an ingredient, source, solvent, formulation, and route-of-administration review. For non-cannabis botanical sources, the CCB requests a natural declaration, manufacturer solvent statements, extraction-solvent list, a detailed COA, and an SOP specifying ingredient percentages. Synthetic ingredients are denied; listed carriers or ingredients are not approved; terpene content is capped at ten percent of the final product.

### Necessary correction

“The terpene that went back in” is too narrow and may falsely imply that all added terpenes were first removed from the same cannabis lot. The public guidance covers terpene blends from cannabis and other natural or botanical sources. A product-specific story must identify whether terpenes were recovered, separately sourced from cannabis, botanically sourced, or blended from multiple origins.

### Why it does not win now

This is the most scientifically consequential alternate, but the public record cannot establish inhalation safety for a particular formulation. The guidance itself notes the lack of FDA inactive-ingredient classification for inhaled terpenes and uses occurrence in cannabis plus a concentration ceiling as its regulatory rationale. Translating that into a public-health conclusion requires aerosol chemistry and inhalation-toxicology review, product-specific composition, heating conditions, emissions data, and careful treatment of dose. It should not be rushed into the fourteenth slot merely because the title is compelling.

## Selection logic

Kief-enhanced usable cannabis is recommended because it offers the best combination of:

1. **Nevada specificity:** a new May 2026 control architecture effective June 1, 2026.
2. **Technology centrality:** physical separation and recombination are coupled to digital classification, processing, lot traceability, and laboratory sampling.
3. **Public revelation:** the material can become more concentrated without legally becoming an infused product, while its data identity must become more specific.
4. **Accountability:** the record permits comparison among declared source weights, finished weight, lot presentation, sampling, and reported potency.
5. **Domain balance:** it adds raw-plant processing and sampling rather than another package, consumer-data, waste, transport, or shelf-life story.
6. **Feasible reporting:** operator, lab, CCB/Metrc, and product-record verification can answer the central questions without requiring the heavier toxicological proof demanded by the terpene story.

The candidate still has meaningful overlap with edible homogeneity. Its full dossier must distinguish **manufacturing a representative raw-plant lot after enhancement** from **correcting a failed edible-production run**. If that distinction cannot sustain a complete general-reader narrative, the candidate must be replaced.
