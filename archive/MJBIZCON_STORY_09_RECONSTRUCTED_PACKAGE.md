# Nevada Reserved Ten Lounge Licenses for Social Equity. Then the Process Kept Going.

In November 2022, a computer selected ten numbers.

Each number represented an application for one of Nevada’s first independent cannabis consumption lounges reserved for social-equity applicants. Thirty complete social-equity applications had entered the pool. Ten were drawn. The selection event was public, mechanical, and easy to narrate: names went in; winners came out.

But nobody had won an operating lounge.

What the applicants received was a place at the beginning of another process. They still had to survive a suitability investigation, document ownership and financing, secure state and local approvals, develop a site, build a compliant facility, pass inspection, obtain final licensure, and open. Nevada’s social-equity lounge experiment is clearest when those stages remain separate. Collapse them into the word “license,” and the most important part of the story disappears.

The distinction began in the law itself. Assembly Bill 341, enacted in 2021, authorized cannabis consumption lounges and created two routes. A retail lounge could be attached or adjacent to an existing adult-use cannabis store. An independent lounge did not require ownership of a dispensary. Of the first twenty independent lounge licenses, at least ten were reserved for applicants meeting Nevada’s social-equity criteria.

That structure addressed a real barrier. Nevada’s licensed cannabis market was already difficult for new entrants to penetrate. An independent-lounge category created a path that did not begin with possession of a retail cannabis license. The reservation rule went further: half of the initial independent opportunities would go to applicants tied, through the regulatory criteria, to places and families affected by deprivation, incarceration, and cannabis enforcement.

Qualification was not a loose promise to consider disadvantage. The Cannabis Compliance Board’s regulations required at least 51 percent ownership and control by the qualifying applicant. They also used address-level measures from two national datasets: an Area Deprivation Index threshold and an incarceration-rate threshold. The applicant also needed a qualifying cannabis conviction, or a qualifying conviction involving an immediate family member, subject to exclusions.

The administrative fee reflected the distinction. Social-equity applicants paid a reduced processing fee. Yet a discount on entry did not remove what came after entry: proof, investigation, capital, property, design, construction, local permission, and time.

The definition also reveals what an administrative system can and cannot measure. Nevada did not ask an application reviewer to decide, in the abstract, whether someone came from a community harmed by drug policy. It translated that judgment into verifiable fields: an ownership percentage, years at an address, a deprivation score, an incarceration statistic, and a conviction relationship. Bright lines can make decisions more consistent and auditable. They can also turn the accuracy of a social policy into the accuracy of an address lookup, a dataset, and a calculation.

The two geographic tools were built for purposes larger than cannabis licensing. The Neighborhood Atlas assigns an Area Deprivation Index using measures of income, education, employment, and housing quality. The Opportunity Atlas traces adult outcomes associated with the census tracts where children grew up, including incarceration. Nevada combined thresholds from both tools with conviction and ownership requirements. The result was not a complete biography of harm. It was a regulatory proxy designed to decide who could enter a reserved pool.

That distinction should follow every number in the program. Passing the criteria meant that an applicant satisfied Nevada’s adopted test; it did not establish that every qualifying person experienced the same harm. Failing the test did not establish an absence of disadvantage. The rule needed a boundary because the state had only ten reserved initial opportunities. The boundary then made small differences in data retrieval or calculation capable of deciding who proceeded.

During the ten-day application window in October 2022, Nevada received 100 complete lounge applications. Twenty were for retail-attached lounges. Fifty were for independent lounges outside the social-equity pool. Thirty were independent social-equity applications.

Because the independent categories were capped, Nevada used random-number selection. On November 30, ten social-equity applications were drawn to proceed. The state’s published list called the recipients “prospective license holders.” That phrase was accurate and consequential. A prospective license was not a conditional license. A conditional license was not final licensure. Final licensure was not proof that the doors had opened or remained open.

The regulatory path worked like a series of narrowing gates.

First came application completeness: submission through the state portal and timely payment of the correct fee. Then minimum eligibility and selection. Next came the suitability investigation, including ownership, financing, and other required information. Applicants who cleared suitability could receive conditional licensure. Conditional status allowed them to continue developing their premises, but opening still required local approval and a successful final state inspection. Only then could a final license support operation.

Each gate asked a different question.

Did the application arrive correctly? Did the applicant meet the bright-line social-equity definition? Was the application selected from an oversubscribed pool? Were the owners and financing suitable? Did the applicant control a viable site? Would the jurisdiction permit it? Was the space built to the ventilation, security, product-control, accessibility, and operational rules? Did it pass inspection?

Calling the first drawing a distribution of ten operating licenses would erase every question after the third one.

The fragility of the early stage became visible in 2024. More than a year after the drawing, the Cannabis Compliance Board reported that none of the ten reserved licenses had yet been issued. An internal audit rechecked the addresses used to establish eligibility. Investigators found that six of the ten selected applications did not satisfy the incarceration-rate component of the residency test because Opportunity Atlas figures had been miscalculated.

The Board did not describe this as six businesses failing in the market. The businesses had not reached the market. It classified six selected applicants as ineligible to proceed.

That difference matters for both fairness and diagnosis. A person can lose a place because an eligibility measure was applied incorrectly, because later evidence changes the record, because financing fails, because a site cannot be secured, because a locality refuses approval, because construction does not finish, or because an inspection is not passed. These events produce the same visible result—no open lounge—but they are not the same failure.

The audit also examined the twenty applications that had not been drawn. At least eight had supplied addresses that met both geographic thresholds at the time of the licensing round. Nevada invited those eight applicants to decide whether to enter a second random selection for the six vacated places. All eight opted in. In August 2024, six were selected to receive prospective licenses.

The state also announced that it would return the $2,500 application fee to social-equity applicants who were not proceeding. That refund acknowledged one burden. It could not reimburse the time spent assembling an application, arranging ownership, seeking capital, planning a venue, or waiting while the administrative record changed.

The next documented advances came one applicant at a time.

In May 2025, Nevada Consumption Lounge LLC became the first social-equity applicant to receive Board approval for a conditional independent-lounge license. The meeting minutes identify owners Natonya Bennett and Jamella Brown and describe their plans for a local-serving venue with entertainment, food, mocktails, consumer education, and trained staff. The Board’s approval did not authorize immediate opening. The minutes expressly preserved local approval and final inspection as later requirements.

In August 2025, the Board conditionally approved Sunflower Compassionate Company LLC. Its owner, Rachel Lee, described entering cannabis through concern for her mother and discussed medicinal education and community programs. At the same meeting, the Board approved a transfer through which outside entities would acquire 49 percent while Lee retained the ownership share necessary for social-equity status. The minutes are unusually revealing about a problem usually flattened into “access to capital”: financing can help an applicant move forward while also changing who owns, funds, and may eventually benefit from the enterprise.

The 51-percent rule protects a formal majority at a specified point. It does not, standing alone, answer every question about control. Debt terms, management agreements, veto rights, leases, intellectual property, personal guarantees, distributions, and later transfers can shape who bears risk and who receives value. None of those arrangements should be presumed improper. They should be measured if the public claim is that a license created durable ownership and community benefit.

This is why the last gate in the pipeline is not opening day. A program can place a qualifying owner on an application and still fail to produce durable control. It can also require outside capital and expertise without stripping the qualifying owner of meaningful authority. The relevant evidence sits in governance and economics, not in the celebratory photograph alone.

Other applicants remained earlier in the pipeline. In March 2026, the Board extended application deadlines for Greenwood Investment Group and Firehouse 420 to March 2027. The record shows continued opportunity, not completion. It also shows how a program announced in 2021 and selected in 2022 could still be processing applicants years later.

By July 1, 2026, Nevada’s public active-license list contained three consumption lounges: Dazed!, Society, and Smoke and Mirrors. Board records identify the operating licenses as retail-attached lounges. The public active list did not show an operating independent social-equity lounge.

That is a dated public-record finding, not a claim that no applicant has made progress beyond the documents reviewed. It does not establish private financing conditions, construction status, unannounced openings, or the motives behind delay. It does establish the distance between reserving ten opportunities and producing ten operating businesses.

The distance is partly built into the policy design. Retail-attached applicants begin with something independent applicants do not: an existing licensed store, its compliance history, premises, customer traffic, procurement relationships, and access to cannabis inventory. An independent lounge must obtain cannabis through the channels permitted by Nevada law while building a venue without the same attached retail platform. Social-equity reservation changes who may enter the independent queue. It does not by itself equalize starting assets across the two lounge types.

Nor does random selection allocate readiness. A lottery can distribute scarce prospective opportunities without pretending that a scoring panel can rank every business plan objectively. It cannot supply capital, property, zoning, design expertise, compliant ventilation, patient investors, or administrative endurance. Randomness can make one stage less discretionary while leaving every later stage unequal.

Nevada’s experience therefore resists two easy conclusions.

The first is that the reservation accomplished nothing. It created a legally protected entry route, reduced fees, placed qualifying applicants into a category that existing dispensary owners could not absorb, and carried at least two applicants to conditional licensure by the dated record.

The second is that reserving ten licenses delivered ten social-equity businesses. The reservation operated at the level of prospective licensing. The lived outcome depended on a chain of later institutions and resources.

That chain is the useful object to save. Application. Eligibility. Selection. Suitability. Conditional license. Local approval. Build-out. Inspection. Final license. Opening. Continued operation. Ownership retention.

When policymakers report social-equity results, every stage needs its own count and date. Otherwise, ten reserved opportunities can be celebrated as ten businesses even when the public can visit none of them. Or a slow pipeline can be condemned as total failure even while applicants are advancing through difficult, visible gates.

The computer selected ten numbers in 2022. Nevada then had to turn selected applications into places with addresses, air systems, employees, inventory, inspections, and open doors. Social equity was not a switch the lottery turned on. It was a promise passed from one gate to the next, with the possibility of narrowing at every handoff.

- Do not say “no social-equity lounge exists” as an undated universal claim. State the exact public-record date and reviewed license crosswalk.
- Do not attribute delay to incompetence, undercapitalization, predatory investment, local obstruction, or applicant fault without evidence tied to a specific case.

- Do not claim 51% ownership guarantees control or benefit without examining governing documents and actual operation.
